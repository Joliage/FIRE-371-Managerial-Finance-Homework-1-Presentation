Chapter 1 · the big picture

Three questions every firm must answer

Capital budgeting

What long-term investments or projects should the business take on? The process of planning and managing long-term assets.

Capital structure

How should we pay for those assets? The mix of debt and equity used to finance the firm.

Working capital

How do we manage day-to-day finances — the short-term assets and liabilities that keep operations running?

The financial manager's domain

These three decisions — from Caleb wondering how to fund more juice stands to any CFO's daily work — are the whole of corporate finance. The first three chapters build the vocabulary and tools to answer them.

Chapter 1 · the goal & the structure

What is the firm trying to do?

The goal of financial management

To maximize the current value per share of existing stock — the owners' wealth. Not simply to maximize profit (which ignores timing and risk), but to maximize value.

Value accounts for the size, timing, and risk of future cash flows — the recurring theme of the whole course.

Three forms of business

  • Sole proprietorship — simple, but unlimited liability and limited life
  • Partnership — shared, still unlimited liability (for general partners)
  • Corporation — limited liability, easy to raise capital, but double taxation and separation of ownership and control
Chapter 1 · Homework Exercise 1.12

The agency problem

When ownership and control are separated, managers (agents) may not always act in the owners' (principals') best interest. This is the agency problem, and it carries agency costs.

Your homework's takeaway: if a manager believes the company is worth more than a $35-per-share buyout offer, acting for shareholders means pushing for a better bid — not accepting the low offer.

Aligning incentives

  • Compensation tied to performance and share price (stock options)
  • The threat of takeovers disciplines underperforming managers
  • Monitoring by boards and large shareholders

Markets and contracts exist to keep managers working for owners — and to reassure investors like Mary after Enron and WorldCom.

Chapter 2 · the two core statements

Balance sheet & income statement

Balance sheet

A snapshot of the firm at a point in time. Assets listed in order of liquidity. The identity always holds:

Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity

Net working capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities — usually positive in a healthy firm.

Income statement

Performance over a period of time. Works down from revenue to the bottom line:

Sales − Costs − Depreciation = EBIT
EBIT − Interest = Taxable income
− Taxes = Net income

Watch for non-cash items (depreciation) and the difference between average and marginal tax rates.

Chapter 2 · the central identity

Cash flow from assets

The most important idea in Chapter 2: the cash a firm generates from its assets must equal the cash it pays out to those who financed it.

CF from Assets = CF to Creditors + CF to Stockholders

And cash flow from assets is built from three pieces:

CFA = OCF − Net Capital Spending − ΔNWC

The component formulas

  • OCF = EBIT + Depreciation − Taxes
  • NCS = ending net fixed assets − beginning + depreciation
  • ΔNWC = ending NWC − beginning NWC
  • CF to creditors = interest − net new borrowing
  • CF to stockholders = dividends − net new equity

Chapter 2 · Homework Problem 14

The full cash-flow identity in action

With sales $178,000, costs $103,600, other $5,100, depreciation $12,100, interest $8,900, taxes $12,705, dividends $10,143, $2,900 new equity, $4,000 debt redeemed:

QuantityValue
a) OCF$56,595
b) CF to creditors8,900 − (−4,000) = $12,900
c) CF to stockholders10,143 − 2,900 = $7,243
d) ΔNWC$1,212

How (d) falls out

CFA = CF to creditors + CF to stockholders = 12,900 + 7,243 = $20,143.

NCS = depreciation + increase in fixed assets = 12,100 + 23,140 = $35,240.

Then ΔNWC = OCF − NCS − CFA = 56,595 − 35,240 − 20,143 = $1,212. All correct. ✓

Chapter 3 · ratio analysis

Five families of financial ratios

Liquidity

Short-term solvency — can the firm pay its bills? Current ratio, quick ratio.

Leverage

Long-term solvency — debt management. Debt ratio, times interest earned.

Turnover

Asset management — how efficiently assets generate sales. Inventory & receivables turnover.

Profitability

Profit margin, ROA, ROE — how much profit per dollar of sales, assets, equity.

Why standardize first?

Common-size statements express every balance-sheet item as a percent of total assets, and every income-statement line as a percent of sales. That lets you compare firms of different sizes — and the same firm over time — on equal footing before computing ratios.

Chapter 3 · the key decomposition

The DuPont identity

The DuPont identity breaks ROE into three levers, showing why a firm earns the return it does:

ROE = Profit Margin × Total Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier
  • Profit margin — operating efficiency
  • Asset turnover — asset-use efficiency
  • Equity multiplier — financial leverage

The building blocks

ROA = Net Income / Total Assets
ROE = Net Income / Total Equity
Equity Multiplier = Total Assets / Total Equity
ROE = ROA × Equity Multiplier

Two firms with the same ROE can get there very differently — one through margins, another through leverage. The analyzer on the next slide makes the trade-offs visible.

Chapter 3 · how fast can a firm grow?

Internal & sustainable growth

Internal growth rate

The most a firm can grow using only retained earnings — no outside financing at all.

IGR = (ROA × b) / (1 − ROA × b)

Sustainable growth rate

The most a firm can grow using retained earnings plus debt, while keeping a constant debt ratio.

SGR = (ROE × b) / (1 − ROE × b)
In both, b is the plowback (retention) ratio = 1 − payout ratio. Homework: Levine's IGR = 6.21% (ROA 7.8%, payout 25%); Crash Davis's SGR = 13.22% (ROE 14.6%, payout 20%). Both verified. ✓

Chapter 3 · Homework Exercise 29

Reading a misleading claim

A grocery chain advertises: "Send your child with $2 to buy $50 of groceries, and your child makes twice the profit margin we do." Using its statements (sales $685M, net income $13.70M, assets $365M, debt $229.80M):

MeasureValue
Store profit margin13.70/685 = 2%
"Child" margin2/50 = 4%
Total equity365 − 229.80 = 135.2
Store ROE13.70/135.2 = 10.13%

Why the claim misleads

The comparison is profit margin on a single transaction vs. the firm's overall margin — ignoring that the store turns its assets over many times a year and earns a healthy 10.13% ROE.

A high margin on one sale isn't the same as a high return on the business. This is exactly why DuPont separates margin from turnover and leverage. All figures verified. ✓
